## The Money Nigerian Companies Leave on the Runway: Why Business Travel Delays in Europe Are a Finance Problem, Not Just a Traveller's Problem

*Every week, Nigerian executives sit in Lagos, London, Amsterdam and Paris airports watching a delayed or cancelled flight quietly cost their employer hundreds of euros in compensation that almost no company ever claims.*

### A cost that never reaches the balance sheet

Nigerian firms with a European footprint, banks, oil and gas majors, telecoms, professional services, shipping, send staff on European routes constantly. A director flying Lagos to Amsterdam for a board meeting. A procurement lead connecting through Paris to a supplier site. A compliance officer routed via London for a regulatory audit. These are not leisure trips with flexible timing. A three-hour delay can mean a missed meeting, a missed connection, a lost negotiating window, or a hotel night nobody budgeted for.

What almost none of these companies realise is that many of these delays trigger a legal right to fixed cash compensation, paid directly for the disruption itself, separate from any refund or rebooking the airline already offered. This is not a grey area or a goodwill gesture. It is regulation, with fixed amounts, and it applies whether the traveller was in economy or business class, whether the ticket was paid by the company or the individual, and regardless of the passenger's nationality.

The amounts are not trivial at scale. A mid-sized Nigerian company running twenty to thirty European trips a month, across a sales team, an executive layer and technical staff, will statistically generate several eligible delays or cancellations every quarter. Most of that money is never claimed, not because the company is not entitled to it, but because nobody in the travel or finance department knows the entitlement exists, and the claim window closes over time.

### Two regulations, one confusing continent

This is where most Nigerian travel managers get it wrong, and where the confusion costs real money. There is no single "Europe rule." Which regulation applies, and how much it pays, depends on the flag of the operating airline and the airport of departure, not on the passenger's route familiarity or nationality.

European Union Regulation 261/2004 applies in two situations: any flight departing from an airport in the European Union, on any airline, or a flight departing from outside the EU and landing in the EU, but only if the operating carrier is an EU-registered airline, such as Air France, KLM or Lufthansa. Under this regulation, a Lagos to Amsterdam flight operated by KLM qualifies. Compensation is fixed by distance: 250 euros for the shortest flights, 400 euros for medium-haul and intra-EU routes, and 600 euros for long-haul flights over 3 500 kilometres, which is where most Lagos-Europe routes fall.

The United Kingdom left this system when it left the EU, but it kept its own version of the rule, commonly referred to as UK261, with its own separate compensation scale in pounds sterling, roughly 220, 350 or 520 pounds depending on distance, not the same figures as the EU regulation. A Lagos to London flight operated by British Airways falls under UK261, not EU261. A Lagos to London flight operated by a non-EU, non-UK carrier may fall under neither regime through this mechanism at all.

In practice, this means two employees on the same corporate trip, delayed on the same day, can have entirely different entitlements, or none, purely because of which airline's metal they were sitting in and which airport their itinerary touched. A travel policy that says "we fly Europe on Airline X for the loyalty points" without checking whether that carrier is EU- or UK-flagged is, without knowing it, deciding whether a delay is worth 600 euros, 520 pounds, or nothing.

### Not automatic, and not always available

Airlines are not required to pay when the disruption stems from what the regulation calls extraordinary circumstances, broadly, events outside the airline's control such as severe weather, security incidents or strikes by third parties. What does not qualify, under a rule the Court of Justice of the European Union settled in the Wallentin-Hermann case, is a technical or mechanical fault with the aircraft. Airlines routinely cite "technical issues" to decline compensation; the case law says that excuse does not hold up. This distinction alone is where a large share of legitimate corporate claims currently die, simply because nobody on the finance side pushes back with the right legal basis.

### A governance gap, not an employee's private matter

Here is the structural problem. When an employee's flight is delayed on a personal holiday, they might chase the claim themselves out of frustration. When the same employee is travelling on company business, on a company-booked ticket, the claim sits in a grey zone. The individual has little incentive to spend hours on it for money that, strictly, belongs to the company that paid for the ticket. The company, meanwhile, has no process to even flag that a claim exists, because travel and finance teams are not trained to look for it. The result is that corporate claims are actually claimed less often than leisure ones, despite corporate travellers flying more, on tighter, more delay-prone schedules, and on higher fare classes where compensation is calculated the same regardless of cabin.

This is squarely a finance and travel-policy question. A company processing dozens of European trips a month should treat flight disruption compensation the way it treats any other recoverable cost: tracked, filed, and reconciled, not left to an individual traveller's goodwill after the fact.

### What this should look like in practice

Corporate travel and finance teams operating routes into the EU or UK do not need to become aviation lawyers. They need three habits. First, know which regulation, if any, applies to each recurring route and carrier combination, since the flag of the airline decides this, not the destination alone. Second, log every delay or cancellation of three hours or more as a potential claim at the time it happens, while boarding passes and booking references are still easy to retrieve. Third, use a specialised recovery service that works on a no-win, no-fee basis, so the company advances nothing and the process costs the finance department only administrative time. Services in this space typically operate through an assignment of the claim rather than a power of attorney, and return the large majority of the compensation, commonly around three-quarters, to the client once the airline pays, with no upfront cost if the claim does not succeed.

None of this requires new legislation or lobbying. The regulations already exist, they have existed for two decades in the EU case, and Nigerian carriers' European partners are already bound by them on qualifying routes. What is missing is simply institutional awareness, the recognition that a delayed flight is not only a scheduling inconvenience for the traveller, but an unclaimed receivable sitting on nobody's books.
