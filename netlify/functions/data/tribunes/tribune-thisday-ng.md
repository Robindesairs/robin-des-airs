## The Refund First-Class Flyers Never Ask For

*A Lagos executive bumped from business class to economy on a flight to Europe is, by law, owed a cash refund that can dwarf the standard delay compensation any economy passenger receives. Almost none of them ask for it.*

There is a quiet irony in how Nigeria's premium travellers relate to European air passenger rights. The businessman who pays a six-figure naira fare to fly business class to London or Paris is statistically far less likely to pursue compensation for a disrupted flight than the passenger in the back of the plane who paid a fraction of that price. The assumption seems to be that EU Regulation 261/2004 is a modest consumer protection for modest tickets, not something that concerns a traveller who has already paid a premium for comfort and reliability. That assumption is wrong, and it is costing premium flyers money that is, per passenger, often larger than what economy travellers are entitled to.

### A flat right, regardless of the fare paid

EU261 grants fixed compensation to any passenger, in any cabin, whose flight arrives more than three hours late, is cancelled, or who is denied boarding, when the flight qualifies under the regulation. The amount depends only on distance: 250 euros up to 1 500 kilometres, 400 euros for flights between 1,500 and 3 500 kilometres, and 600 euros beyond that. A Lagos-to-Europe route typically falls in the 600-euro tier. Crucially, the figure does not change whether the passenger sat in economy or in a fully flat business-class seat. A first-class ticket confers no larger entitlement under this particular rule, which is precisely why many premium travellers dismiss it: 600 euros can feel beneath the trouble for someone who has just spent several thousand.

### The claim premium flyers actually overlook

What most premium travellers do not realise is that a separate, considerably more valuable right exists specifically for them. Under Article 10 of the same regulation, a passenger who is placed in a lower class than the one paid for, an involuntary downgrade, is owed a cash reimbursement calculated as a percentage of the price of that specific flight: 30 percent on shorter distances, 50 percent on medium-haul routes, and 75 percent on long-haul flights such as Lagos to Europe. The airline must pay this within seven days, in cash, not as a travel voucher, and without any requirement for the passenger to prove financial loss. The Court of Justice of the European Union, in its Mennens ruling, confirmed that this percentage applies to the price of the affected flight segment alone, not the full itinerary, and generally excludes taxes unless those taxes themselves varied by class. On a business-class fare that can run into the thousands of euros, 75 percent of that single segment can comfortably exceed the standard delay compensation, sometimes by several multiples.

### Why the excuse that usually protects airlines does not apply here

Airlines routinely defend against delay and cancellation claims by invoking "extraordinary circumstances", a defence the CJEU set out in its Wallentin-Hermann ruling, covering events such as severe weather or unforeseen safety faults. That defence has no bearing whatsoever on the downgrade refund. Whether a passenger was downgraded because of a broken seat, a last-minute aircraft swap, or any operational decision by the airline, the refund is still due. Legally, it is not compensation for a disruption; it is a reimbursement for a service that was sold and not delivered. An airline cannot argue its way out of it the way it can with a delay.

### Where the coverage line actually falls

For Nigerian travellers, the rule that decides whether a claim exists at all is the point of departure and the operating carrier. A flight leaving an EU airport is covered under EU261 regardless of which airline operates it. A flight leaving Nigeria towards Europe is covered only if the operating carrier is registered in the EU, which in practice means airlines such as Air France, KLM or Lufthansa, and not the flight's marketing carrier if that carrier is non-European. This distinction matters more, not less, for business travellers, who tend to fly precisely these European long-haul carriers on corporate and diplomatic routes.

The pattern that emerges is straightforward. The passengers with the least time, the least appetite for paperwork, and the most reason to assume "someone will have already sorted it" are the same passengers who are owed the largest individual sums.
