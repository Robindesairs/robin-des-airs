## Accra to London or Accra to Amsterdam? The European Airport You Pick Can Decide Whether a Delay Ever Gets Compensated

*Two Ghanaian travellers, same delayed connection, same misery in the transit lounge — but only one of them may have a right to money back, and it depends on a choice most people make without thinking: which European city, and which airline, they routed through.*

Every week, thousands of Ghanaians fly out of Kotoka heading for Europe, North America or beyond, and almost all of them pass through a European hub first. Amsterdam, Paris, London, Brussels, Istanbul, Addis Ababa — the connection is treated as a formality, a place to stretch your legs before the next leg. Few travellers realise that the hub they choose, and the airline that operates the flight into it, quietly determines what legal protection they carry if that flight is delayed, cancelled or overbooked. This is not a technicality for lawyers. It is a practical question every Ghanaian booking a ticket to Europe should understand, because it can be worth several hundred euros or pounds the difference between a wasted day and a paid one.

### Two Rulebooks, Two Currencies

Europe actually runs two separate compensation systems for air passengers, and they are not identical twins.

The European Union operates Regulation (EC) 261/2004, generally known as EU261. It pays fixed compensation of 250, 400 or 600 euros depending on flight distance, when a flight is delayed by three hours or more at arrival, cancelled with short notice, or when a passenger is denied boarding due to overbooking — unless the airline can prove the disruption was caused by "extraordinary circumstances" genuinely outside its control.

Since Brexit, the United Kingdom runs its own, separate version of the same idea, often called UK261. It works on comparable principles but pays in pounds sterling, at different thresholds: roughly £220, £350 or £520 depending on distance. It is not EU261 with a currency conversion. It is a distinct legal framework, administered under UK law, for flights connected to the UK.

Knowing which one applies to your ticket — or whether either applies at all — starts with the airport and the airline you choose.

### The Amsterdam or Paris Route: EU Protection Follows the Carrier

If a Ghanaian traveller flies Accra to Amsterdam on KLM, or Accra to Paris on Air France, and that flight is delayed, EU261 protection applies. The rule is straightforward: EU261 covers any flight, departing from anywhere in the world, arriving in the European Union, as long as it is operated by an airline registered in the EU. It also covers any flight departing an EU airport, on any airline at all, going anywhere. Since Accra-Amsterdam and Accra-Paris routes are typically well over 3 500 kilometres, a qualifying delay of three hours or more at arrival can trigger 600 euros in compensation, per passenger, regardless of the ticket price paid.

### The London Route: A Different Rulebook, and Sometimes a Gap

Now take the traveller who books Accra to London instead. If that flight is operated by British Airways, a UK-registered carrier, UK261 applies — a real right, but a different one, capped in pounds rather than euros, and enforced through UK institutions rather than EU ones.

Here is the part that surprises most passengers: if the Accra-London flight is instead operated by a carrier that is neither UK-registered nor EU-registered, it can fall outside both frameworks entirely. EU261 does not reach it, because the flight is not arriving in the EU and is not operated by an EU carrier. UK261 was built to mirror EU261's logic for UK-connected flights on UK or EU airlines, so a flight sitting outside that description may leave a passenger with neither specific consumer-compensation right to point to. The disruption is exactly as real. The legal path to compensation is not.

This is not about one airline being better or worse than another in the air. It is purely a question of jurisdiction and registration — the small print of where a carrier is licensed decides which rulebook, if any, is switched on.

### Distance and the Excuse Airlines Cannot Use

Under EU261, distance sets the amount: 250 euros for short flights under 1 500 kilometres, 400 euros for medium-haul or any intra-EU flight, and 600 euros for long-haul routes over 3 500 kilometres outside the EU — which is where most Accra-Europe legs land. Airlines can escape payment only for genuine "extraordinary circumstances": severe weather, security incidents, strikes by third parties. What does not count, according to the EU's Wallentin-Hermann ruling, is a technical or mechanical fault with the aircraft. An engine problem discovered at the gate is treated as part of the airline's ordinary operating risk, not an excuse.

### A Checklist Before You Book

Before choosing a European connection out of Accra, it is worth asking three quick questions: Which country is the connecting airport in? Which airline actually operates that leg — check the registration, not just the codeshare name on your ticket? And is the final destination inside the EU, inside the UK, or neither? The answers determine which compensation regime, if any, is watching over your journey.

None of this requires giving up convenience or price for protection — Amsterdam, Paris and London are all workable choices. It simply means knowing, before disruption happens, which door you can knock on afterward.

### What to Do If a Flight Is Disrupted

Passengers who qualify rarely claim, mainly because pursuing an airline through correspondence and, if necessary, court proceedings feels like more trouble than it is worth for a single delayed evening. Services built specifically for this exist for that reason, working on a no-win-no-fee basis so the passenger risks nothing to find out if they qualify.
