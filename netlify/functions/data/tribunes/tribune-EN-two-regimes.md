## Two passengers, one delay, different rights

*Two travellers check in side by side at Lagos. Same destination, same three-hour delay. One is owed a fixed payment. The other is owed nothing at all. The difference is not the country, and it is not the airline's service. It is the flag it flies under.*

Most African travellers heading to Europe assume that a long delay works the same way everywhere. It does not. Two entirely separate systems apply, one European and one British, and a third possibility applies when neither of them does. Knowing which one covers your ticket is worth several hundred pounds or euros per passenger, and almost nobody is told at the airport.

### The rule that decides everything

Under European Regulation (EC) 261/2004, two branches matter.

**Any flight departing from an airport inside the European Union is covered, whatever the airline.** A delayed Amsterdam to Lagos flight qualifies even if the carrier is African. This branch is the widest and the simplest to remember: if you take off from Europe, you are protected.

**A flight departing from outside the EU towards Europe is covered only if the operating carrier is European.** This is where most passengers are caught out.

The practical consequences are blunt. Lagos to Amsterdam on KLM opens a fixed entitlement of **600 euros per passenger** for a delay of more than three hours on arrival. The same route on a Nigerian carrier opens nothing under EC 261. Accra to Brussels on Brussels Airlines qualifies. Nairobi to Paris on Air France qualifies. And there are far more of these routes than most people realise: Amsterdam, Paris, Brussels, Frankfurt, Munich, Lisbon and Madrid are all served from anglophone Africa by European carriers.

The word that matters is fixed. You do not have to prove that you spent a single naira, cedi or shilling. The entitlement arises from the delay itself.

### London is a different country, legally speaking

Since Brexit, the United Kingdom applies its own retained version of the same rules, commonly called UK261. The structure is familiar but the currency and the amounts are not.

| Flight distance | UK261 | EC 261 |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 1,500 km | £220 | €250 |
| 1,500 to 3,500 km | £350 | €400 |
| Over 3,500 km | £520 | €600 |

Lagos to London and Accra to London are both well over 3,500 km, so the figure is **£520**, reduced to £260 where rerouting keeps the arrival delay under four hours.

The same flag rule applies. Lagos to London on British Airways or Virgin Atlantic is covered by UK261. Nairobi to London on British Airways is covered. The identical route on an African carrier is covered by neither UK261 nor EC 261.

Anyone quoting 600 euros for a London route is simply wrong, and anyone quoting UK261 for an Amsterdam route is wrong in the other direction.

### When neither applies

A passenger outside both systems is not without recourse, but the logic changes completely.

The Montreal Convention, which almost every African state has ratified, makes the carrier liable under its **Article 19** for damage caused by delay. There is no fixed sum. You recover what you actually spent, on receipts: the rebooked ticket, meals during the wait, the hotel if you paid for it yourself, the extra transport. Keep everything, because an undocumented expense simply does not exist.

Some countries add their own layer. Nigeria's Civil Aviation Regulations 2023 include a consumer protection part enforced by the NCAA, built on percentages of the fare and on assistance obligations rather than on fixed compensation. Ghana has directives of a similar spirit. Kenya, at the time of writing, still has no consumer protection regulations in force, only a complaints procedure at the KCAA.

### The trap nobody mentions

This is the part that costs people the most, and it applies precisely to those who fall back on the Montreal Convention.

Article 35 gives you **two years** from the date of arrival, or the date the aircraft should have arrived. After that, the right is not merely harder to enforce. It is extinguished.

And here is what makes it dangerous. That two-year period is not an ordinary limitation period. It is a preclusion. An ordinary limitation period restarts when you send a formal letter of claim. A preclusion does not. It runs to its end regardless of how many letters you write, and only proceedings actually filed before a court will stop the clock.

So a family that writes to an airline patiently for two years, in good faith, believing it is protecting its position, has protected nothing at all. Prolonged silence from a carrier is not always administrative slowness.

### What to do

Find the exact date of the flight, not the month. That date starts the clock.

Check who actually operated the flight, not who sold you the ticket. Codeshares mean the name on your booking is often not the carrier that flew you, and the flag of the operating carrier is what determines your rights.

Keep the proof of purchase. It quantifies your loss, and it can also determine where you are entitled to sue.

And if your flight is more than eighteen months old and departed from outside Europe, stop writing to the airline and check your deadline first.

That last point is not a procedural detail. It is often the only thing standing between a claim you can still recover and a right that has quietly expired.
